The Denver International Airport File: Every Weird Thing That Is Actually There, Where the Runway Swastika and the Underground Reptilian Base Come From, and Why the Airport Has Spent the Past Decade Selling Its Own Conspiracies Back to You

The Denver International Airport opened February 28, 1995, sixteen months behind schedule and roughly two billion dollars over budget, on a 34,000-acre parcel of land twenty-five miles northeast of downtown Denver, which is almost twice the physical footprint of Manhattan for an airport that at the time served a metro area of about two million people. Almost from the day the ribbon got cut, a specific body of conspiracy material started attaching itself to the place, and the material had this useful property that a lot of the raw ingredients were not made up. There really are murals of a gas-masked soldier standing over dead children painted on the interior walls. There really is a plaque in the main terminal that credits construction to the "New World Airport Commission" and carries a Masonic square and compass. There really is a thirty-two foot blue fiberglass mustang with glowing red eyes at the entrance to the airport property, and it really did kill its own sculptor when a piece of it fell on him in 2006. The airport is genuinely enormous, the construction was genuinely bungled, and the art program was genuinely selected by a public arts commission that appears to have been trying to make an artistic statement and instead accidentally created a permanent tourist attraction for people who are certain the whole facility sits on top of a Deep Underground Military Base for continuity of government operations. Then, somewhere around 2018, the airport itself started leaning into it, running marketing campaigns that treat every conspiracy as canon, hanging construction-fence signage that references lizard people, and giving the whole thing a talking-gargoyle mascot. Let me walk through what is actually there, where the specific claims come from, and why an airport built to solve a runway-icing problem in the Colorado high plains has become the single most heavily conspiracy-coded piece of civil aviation infrastructure in North America.

OK. Denver International Airport. The one you have connected through if you have flown domestically west of the Mississippi at any point in the past thirty years. Opened February 28, 1995. Situated on 34,000 acres of former farmland twenty-five miles northeast of downtown Denver, which makes it, in terms of pure land area, the largest airport in North America and the second largest in the world after King Fahd International in Saudi Arabia. Cost roughly 4.8 billion dollars to build. Was originally supposed to open October 1993 and slipped four separate times before it actually opened in February 1995. Was originally supposed to cost about 1.7 billion dollars. Every one of those numbers is real, and every one of those numbers has been folded into the conspiracy at one point or another. Let me walk through what is actually there, what is not there, and why a regional-airport replacement project in Colorado ended up as the single most heavily conspiracy-coded piece of civil aviation infrastructure in the country.

Why The Airport Got Built In The First Place

Before there was Denver International, there was Stapleton International, sitting inside the city limits and hemmed in on all four sides by residential Denver. Stapleton had two problems that were escalating fast in the eighties. The first was noise. The airport was surrounded by neighborhoods, the neighborhoods had lawyers, and every runway expansion proposal ran into a wall of environmental review. The second was weather. Stapleton's runway layout was such that when winter storms rolled through, only one direction of takeoffs and landings was operationally usable, which meant that in bad weather Stapleton became a chokepoint that could snarl domestic aviation nationwide, given that Denver sits in the middle of the country and a substantial share of transcontinental traffic routes through it. Federal Aviation Administration reports through the mid-eighties were pretty explicit that Stapleton needed to be replaced or expanded, and Denver could not expand it, so the city started planning a replacement.

The 34,000-acre parcel out past the airport at Adams County was chosen because it was cheap, because it was flat, and because the prevailing wind pattern meant runways could be laid out so that at least three runways would be usable in any weather. That is the operational reason the airport is on that specific piece of land, that specific size, that far from downtown. It was overbuilt on purpose, so that it would have room to grow for fifty years without repeating Stapleton's problem. The bond issues to pay for it were controversial at every stage. The construction went badly. The famous automated baggage system, contracted to BAE Systems and originally supposed to handle every bag in the airport automatically without human intervention, never worked, went massively over budget, and got quietly ripped out in phases starting a couple of years after opening. That is the single largest reason the airport opened sixteen months late and 2 billion dollars over budget. Not because of what was being built underneath the airport. Because of a baggage system.

The Murals

OK. Here we go. The murals. These are the single most famous element of the DIA conspiracy and they are the reason most people who have never been to Denver still know the airport by reputation. There are two of them and they are painted on interior walls near the baggage claim on level five. They were done by a Chicano-American muralist named Leo Tanguma, who was commissioned as part of the airport's public art program, which spent about 7.5 million dollars total on public art across the facility. Tanguma's two panels are titled "Children of the World Dream of Peace" and "In Peace and Harmony with Nature."

Here is what is actually in them. The first panel shows a large central figure of a soldier in a green gas mask, carrying a sword and a rifle, standing over what look like weeping mothers holding dead children. Around the perimeter of the panel, children of many nationalities are gathered around a globe. Then the panel resolves, on the second half, into an image of children beating swords into plowshares and the gas-masked figure being dismantled. It is composed as a before-and-after. The gas-masked figure is meant to represent war, is defeated by the children, and the second half of the panel is the resulting peace.

The second panel does the same structural move with environmental destruction. First half shows animals in glass vitrines, mothers weeping over dead children, plants dying, a city burning. Second half shows a rainforest restored, animals released, children planting trees. It is Tanguma's standard idiom. If you look at his other public work, all over Denver and Houston and everywhere he worked in the seventies and eighties, this is exactly what his murals do. There is a violent image, then a redemption arc, then a peace resolution.

The conspiracy read strips out the second half. It photographs the gas-masked soldier and the dead children and none of the resolution. It presents the image as a plan, not a warning. This is genuinely how the conspiracy circulates. If you look at the images that go viral on TikTok and Instagram, they are almost always cropped to the first half of the panel. If you go to the airport and look at the mural in person, the second half is right there, on the same wall, and the gas-masked figure is being taken apart by children with hammers. The mural is arguably not subtle about being anti-war and anti-environmental-destruction. It is subtle-adjacent, but not in the direction the conspiracy needs.

There is a separate and more defensible complaint about the murals, which is that they are visually intense in a way that is unusual for an airport terminal, and that plenty of travelers find them unsettling to encounter unexpectedly on their way to baggage claim. That is a valid critique of the curatorial choice. It is a different critique than the one that says the murals are a coded announcement of a coming genocide by the New World Order. The first is aesthetics. The second requires you to ignore roughly half of each mural.

The Blue Mustang

Blucifer. Officially titled "Blue Mustang." Thirty-two feet tall, blue fiberglass, glowing red LED eyes, rearing up on its hind legs at the entrance to the airport property. You cannot drive to the terminal without seeing it. Sculpted by Luis Jimenez, a Mexican-American sculptor from El Paso who was one of the more accomplished large-scale fiberglass sculptors working in the United States in the late twentieth century. The commission was awarded in 1992. Jimenez worked on the piece intermittently through the nineties and into the 2000s.

In June 2006, while Jimenez was in his studio in Hondo, New Mexico working on the mustang, a section of the head came loose from the hoist that was holding it up and fell on him. It severed an artery in his leg. He bled out before he could get to a hospital. He was sixty-five. His family and studio assistants finished the piece using his existing molds and specifications, and it was installed at DIA in February 2008.

The red eyes are not a conspiracy addition. They are part of the artist's design. Jimenez used red eyes on multiple pieces of his work throughout his career, in reference to his father's neon sign business in El Paso, where his father had made a horse sign with red neon eyes that Luis had grown up with. The red eyes at DIA are, essentially, a tribute to his own father. That is on the public record. Jimenez talked about it in interviews going back to the eighties.

The fact that the sculptor was killed by his own sculpture is real. The fact that the sculpture has red glowing eyes is real. The fact that the sculpture is at the entrance to an airport is real. The combination is genuinely weird. The conspiracy read of that combination, which is that the horse is a placement of the pale horse from Revelation and that the death of the sculptor was a ritual sacrifice, is a leap the raw facts do not by themselves require, but it is the leap a lot of the internet has decided to make, and once made, that leap is very hard to walk back.

The Capstone

There is a granite dedication plaque on the floor of the main terminal at DIA. It is set into the floor and travelers walk over it constantly. It reads, in relevant part, that the airport was built by the "New World Airport Commission," lists a dedication date of March 19, 1994, and carries a Masonic square and compass symbol along with an inscription about a time capsule to be opened in 2094.

The New World Airport Commission was a real organization. It was a promotional group organized to raise money for the airport's opening ceremonies. It was disbanded shortly after the airport opened. The name is unfortunate in a sounds-like-New-World-Order way, but it does not appear to have been chosen with any conspiratorial intent. It was a promotional entity for a new world-class airport, and the phrase "world-class" was doing a lot of work in nineties American infrastructure branding.

The square and compass is a real Masonic symbol. The plaque was placed by the Grand Lodge of Colorado, and Masonic lodges routinely place dedication plaques at civic infrastructure projects, because that is part of what Masonic lodges have done historically, going back to the founding of the country. There are hundreds of similar plaques on hundreds of American civic buildings. What is different about the DIA plaque is that it is at an airport, on the floor, and the airport also has a giant blue horse and gas-masked murals, and the aggregation makes the individual plaque read as significantly more sinister than it would if it were on the side of a small-town courthouse in Missouri.

The 2094 time capsule reference is also real. Denver-area schoolchildren contributed items to the capsule during construction and it is scheduled to be opened on the airport's hundredth anniversary. There is no public information about what is in it beyond the schoolchildren's contributions, which is somewhat the point of time capsules.

The Gargoyles

There are two bronze gargoyles at DIA, one at each of the east and west baggage claim areas, each perched on top of a suitcase. They are collectively titled "Notre Denver" and they were sculpted by Terry Allen, a Lubbock-based artist who does a lot of narrative sculpture. The joke is that they are the airport's watchers over lost luggage. Allen has said in interviews that the pieces are meant to be a wry commentary on how much time modern travelers spend anxious about their bags. They are also visually a lot, in a way that a lot of airport art is not, and they photograph well, and they have become the second most photographed pieces of art at DIA after the murals.

The conspiracy read on the gargoyles is that they are ceremonial guardians of an underground temple to something-or-other. The gargoyles themselves are just sculptures on top of suitcases. But they are weird sculptures on top of suitcases, and they are at the airport that has the murals and the horse and the plaque, and that is enough.

The Underground Tunnels

Here is where the conspiracy leaves the observable and enters the speculative. The claim, in circulation since roughly the late nineties and codified across a hundred different YouTube videos since, is that DIA sits on top of a network of underground tunnels that extend for miles beneath the airport, that these tunnels connect to a DUMB (Deep Underground Military Base) associated with continuity-of-government operations, and that whatever is down there is either NORAD-adjacent, a bunker for the elite in the event of civil collapse, a reptilian breeding facility, or some combination of the three.

The observable fact behind this is that DIA does have an underground component. Every large modern airport does. There are tunnels for the train that connects the concourses. There are utility tunnels for HVAC, electrical, water, and communications runs. There are baggage tunnels, or there were until the automated system got ripped out, and even now there is a subterranean baggage transfer system that runs under the concourses. There are rooms for airport operations, security operations, and the various federal agencies (TSA, CBP, FBI) that maintain presence at any major international airport. All of this is normal airport infrastructure. All of this is visible on architectural drawings that are part of the public record from the airport's construction.

What is not on any public record is any Deep Underground Military Base. The DUMB claim originates with a specific set of nineties-era conspiracy authors, most prominently Phil Schneider, a self-described former government engineer who gave a series of lectures in the mid-nineties claiming to have participated in the construction of underground bases across the American West, including at Dulce, New Mexico and Denver. Schneider claimed to have fought reptilians in the Dulce tunnels and to have knowledge of an equivalent facility beneath DIA. He died in January 1996 under circumstances his family and supporters called suspicious. The medical examiner ruled the death a suicide. His lectures survived him and have been the substrate of the DIA-DUMB claim ever since.

The specific evidence for a DUMB beneath DIA is that some construction workers on the airport project reported multiple large buildings being built and then buried during the construction phase, which airport officials have described as buildings that had construction problems and were built over during subsequent phases. This is a genuinely unusual construction detail. It is also not by itself evidence of anything more than that a project with the scale of DIA had construction problems and some of the problems were solved by burying them. The distance between "there is stuff buried under the airport" and "there is a multi-story government facility under the airport housing continuity of government operations and reptilian activity" is a distance the physical evidence does not close.

The Runway Swastika

The claim is that if you look at the runway layout of DIA from a satellite view, the six runways form a swastika. They do not. The runways form a shape that is closer to a pinwheel or an asterisk. It is a common airport runway layout designed to allow at least one runway to be usable in any wind direction. If you crop the image aggressively and squint you can talk yourself into a partial swastika. If you look at the actual layout, you see a fairly standard midwestern-United-States airport runway plan.

This one is worth mentioning specifically because it is the easiest DIA conspiracy claim to check. You do not need any insider information. You do not need to trust anybody's testimony. You can pull up Google Maps, look at the airport, and see that the runways do not form a swastika. The claim has continued to circulate anyway, because in the ecosystem of DIA conspiracy content, the runway swastika is a mood, not an empirical claim, and moods are hard to fact-check into submission.

Why This Specific Airport

Every large piece of civil infrastructure attracts some conspiracy content. The Hoover Dam attracts theories about the reliefs on the intake towers. The Georgia Guidestones attracted theories for forty years before somebody blew them up in 2022. The Bohemian Grove attracts an entire subgenre of elite-ritual content (and if you want the full Cremation of Care rundown, it is worth doing in its own right). But DIA has attracted more conspiracy attention per acre than any other piece of civil aviation infrastructure in North America, and the reasons are specific and worth naming.

The first reason is aggregation. Any individual element of the DIA conspiracy could exist in isolation without generating much attention. Weird public art exists in a lot of airports. Masonic dedication plaques exist on a lot of civic buildings. Large horse statues with red eyes are unusual but not unique. Enormous airports with big footprints and long construction delays exist elsewhere. The reason DIA is uniquely conspiracy-coded is that all of these things exist at the same airport, and the mind aggregating them is inclined to look for a unifying explanation that ties them all together. The unifying explanation the aggregating mind produces is a conspiracy, because conspiracies are the shape of narrative that unifies a large number of odd but individually unrelated details.

The second reason is that DIA was built during a specific moment in American conspiracy culture. It opened in February 1995. Ruby Ridge was three years past and had produced a durable militia movement. Waco was two years past. The month after DIA opened, Oklahoma City happened. The nineties were the peak decade of American New World Order conspiracy content, and DIA opened directly into it. If DIA had opened in 1978, before the NWO framework had a name, it would be a weird airport with weird art. Because it opened in 1995, it is the NWO airport.

The third reason is that the murals, unlike almost every other element on the list, are genuinely striking pieces of political art that a lot of people find upsetting to encounter without warning. The airport put them right where every arriving passenger has to walk past them to get to baggage claim. That curatorial choice guaranteed that a huge number of first-time visitors to DIA would have a bad moment in front of the murals, would take a photograph of the bad moment, and would go home and look for an explanation for why an airport was displaying imagery of dead children in gas masks. The explanation the internet handed them was the conspiracy. If the murals had been in a museum, this would not have happened in remotely the same way.

How The Airport Started Selling It Back

Somewhere around 2018, DIA's marketing team made a decision that has genuinely reshaped the airport's relationship with its own reputation. They stopped denying the conspiracies and started monetizing them.

The specific inflection point was a renovation project that walled off large sections of the main terminal for several years of construction. The airport wrapped the construction fences in signage that made explicit references to lizard people, illuminati confirmations, and other conspiracy touchpoints. There is a series of signs that show a gargoyle character speaking directly to travelers about how the "New World Airport Commission is at it again." The airport has a talking gargoyle mascot named Ozzy that appears in advertising and on the airport's social media. Every October the airport runs Halloween programming that leans on its conspiracy content. There is merchandise. There is a website. There is a section of the airport's official visitor materials that walks you through the conspiracies as attractions.

This has been remarkably effective. The airport gets significantly more media coverage than a regional airport hub would otherwise attract. The conspiracy content generates a steady stream of travelers who deliberately layover at DIA to photograph the murals and the horse. The airport is arguably now the best-branded regional airport in the country, and it did it by leaning into content that other airports would have spent millions of dollars trying to make go away.

There is a real critique of this move, which is that DIA has taken content that includes genuine elements of antisemitic conspiracy tradition (the NWO framework, the Masonic-cabal framework, the depopulation narrative all have roots that go through pretty well-documented antisemitic sources) and turned it into a marketing bit. The airport gets to profit from the aesthetic of the conspiracy while the substrate keeps generating harm elsewhere. This is a legitimate criticism that the airport has not, to my knowledge, ever engaged with publicly.

The Version That Is Actually True

Let me consolidate what is real. DIA is a genuinely enormous airport built to replace a genuinely obsolete predecessor on a genuinely large piece of land far from downtown Denver. The construction was genuinely bungled, the delays and cost overruns were real, and the driver of both was the automated baggage system, which was a real engineering failure that has been extensively documented in the trade press and in academic case studies on public procurement. The public art commission was real, the murals are real, the horse is real, the plaque is real, the gargoyles are real. The Masonic dedication is a normal Masonic dedication of a civic building, of which there are hundreds in the United States. The New World Airport Commission was a real promotional body with an unfortunate name.

The underground DUMB, the reptilian base, the continuity of government facility, the runway swastika, the depopulation announcement encoded in the murals, none of these things has evidence anybody has produced that stands up to routine scrutiny. What exists is an aggregation of individually unusual details that a nineties-and-onward conspiracy culture stitched into a unified narrative, because unified narratives are what that culture produces from individually unusual details, and DIA had more individually unusual details in one place than any other airport it could find.

The airport has since made peace with the framework and turned it into a revenue stream. This is genuinely one of the more interesting institutional responses to viral conspiracy content in the modern era. It has upside, in that the airport is now branded in a way that generates attention and travel. It has downside, in that the underlying framework the airport is now profiting from is a framework with a long history of doing real harm to real people, and that history is not going away just because the airport is now selling t-shirts.

Where I Land

The Denver International Airport is not a portal to a subterranean reptilian base. It is a large, weird, badly-built regional airport with an ambitious public art program that made curatorial choices that turned out to have thirty years of unintended second-order consequences. Everything on the observable list is on the observable list. Everything on the speculative list is speculative. The airport is doing a bit about it and the bit is working.

The reason to actually go to DIA and look at the murals is not that they are a coded genocide announcement. It is that they are striking pieces of political art in an unusual and public setting, and that they will make you think about what public art is for and how much of a role a passenger's willingness to be confronted plays in the calculus of putting difficult imagery in a building most travelers are moving through with their headphones on. The reason to look at the horse is that it is a genuinely impressive piece of large-scale fiberglass sculpture by an artist who died completing it. The reason to notice the plaque is that Masonic dedication plaques exist on a lot of American civic buildings and it is worth noticing how many of them you have walked past without incident.

The reason to notice the whole conspiracy is that it is a case study in how an aggregation of unrelated details, deposited into the right cultural moment, will produce a unified narrative that outlives everyone who put the individual details there, and that this narrative is now stable enough that the institution at the center of it has started monetizing it, and that this is now what happens to the raw material of civic infrastructure once it enters the media environment we currently live in.

The airport is on the map. You can go there. Look around. Draw your own conclusions. Then go get your bag from carousel six and go on with your day.
